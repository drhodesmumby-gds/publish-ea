    Development Management

    This page covers the technical architecture supporting development management. The architecture describes the lifecycle of an individual planning application—from the citizen's initial submission to the council's final legal determination.

    Because this service is highly transactional and public-facing, its architecture needs to support high volumes of web traffic, complex spatial rule evaluations, and heavy unstructured document processing. It represents the bulk of a council's planning technology expenditure.

    Who this guidance is for and how to use it

    This page is for local authority enterprise and solution architects, digital and planning service leads, and commissioning or procurement teams. It sets out common patterns and architectural considerations for development management using the LGAM taxonomy — it is reference guidance, not a mandatory blueprint or a single prescribed architecture.

    The LGAM technology stack

    Delivering this journey requires orchestrating multiple components across the LGAM taxonomy. An API-first approach decouples these capabilities so they can be procured or replaced independently, though some councils may use an all-in-one suite that bundles them together.

    Business process

    This section outlines the business process at each phase of a planning application. Expand each phase to compare the neutral baseline architecture against target architectural models.

    The planning application context

    The processing of planning applications is subject to The Town and Country Planning (Development Management Procedure) (England) Order 2015 which covers the processing of a range of statutory applications. Other legislation covers other types of application such as Lawful Development Certificates and Applications for Prior Approval setting out required documents for submission, processing timelines and the fee schedule for which is controlled by government. Alongside these statutory application types are discretionary planning services. The most prominent of which are pre-applications, these allow LPAs to offer advice to applicants prior to a formal application submission. The fees for these are set by the LPA themselves and can vary.

    1 Submission

    Phase 1 Architecture

    • Business Process
    • Anti-patterns
    • Target Architecture

    The process: applicants and agents submit the application, plans, and fee, through various services including the Planning Portal. The council needs to receive the applicant's intent, property details, supporting documents, and the site boundary in a form it can use.

    Relevant LGAM taxonomy nodes:

    CapabilitiesForms SecurityIdentity and access management (IAM) DataDocument and records management
    Anti-pattern: submissions received as documents, not data

    Whether through a generic council web form or a third-party service, the application reaches the council as a flattened PDF with minimal metadata. The machine-readable data is lost on day one, and every later stage has to recover it by hand.

    Anti-pattern: free-text address matching

    Letting applicants type their address instead of requiring a UPRN lookup against the corporate gazetteer. Applications then fail to link to the right property and location in later stages.

    Loading diagram…

    Best practice: receive applications as structured data

    Whatever route the applicant uses, the council should receive the application as structured data aligned to the national planning application data standard, with documents attached rather than standing in for the data. MHCLG is consulting on making this standard mandatory for submission services and councils.

    Where councils run their own submission service, rules-as-code services can go further. The questions adapt to the applicant's answers, the address is matched to a UPRN, the site boundary is drawn on a map, relevant constraints are shown early, and the fee is calculated before the applicant pays. Applicants can correct simple errors before they submit.

    Real-world impact
    • Usable data from day one

      Later stages can use what the applicant provided instead of re-keying it.

    • Better applicant experience

      Applicants see the questions that apply to them and know the correct fee before they pay.

    2 Validate and register

    Phase 2 Architecture

    • Business Process
    • Anti-patterns
    • Target Architecture

    The process: ensuring the submission is legally complete, geographically sound, and financially settled before assessment begins. The application is validated, constraints are checked, and it is registered, allocated to a case officer, and acknowledged. Technical support officers carry out validation and registration, and team leaders allocate the work.

    Relevant LGAM taxonomy nodes:

    CapabilitiesWorkflow CapabilitiesPayments DataGeospatial data
    Anti-pattern: swivel-chair integration

    Validation officers re-keying details and redrawing red line boundaries into separate systems, because submission, GIS, and case management systems do not share data.

    Anti-pattern: validation by correspondence

    Checks only start once an application reaches the queue. Officers then email applicants for missing or incorrect information, and applications wait until they reply.

    Loading diagram…

    Best practice: reuse submitted data, and spend officer time on judgement

    Structured application data should flow straight into case management and GIS, with the site boundary recorded without redrawing. Automated checks against national and local validation requirements confirm what can be confirmed: completeness, fee, and constraints. Officers see the results and focus on what needs judgement, such as whether plans are adequate and consistent, and whether ownership certificates are correct. Registration and acknowledgement follow automatically, and team leaders allocate cases.

    Real-world impact
    • Operational efficiency

      Less time spent re-keying data and corresponding with applicants to get applications ready for assessment.

    • Better applicant experience

      Applications are registered sooner, giving applicants more confidence in timelines.

    3 Consultation & Assessment

    Phase 3 Architecture

    • Business Process
    • Anti-patterns
    • Target Architecture

    The process: The core officer workflow. The application is published on the public register, neighbours and statutory consultees, such as the Environment Agency and the highway authority, are consulted, and comments are received. The case officer visits the site, assesses the application against policy and site constraints, negotiates amendments, and writes the officer report.

    Relevant LGAM taxonomy nodes:

    CapabilitiesWorkflow InterfacesWebsites IntegrationPartner (B2B) integration
    Anti-pattern: public register sharing the officers' system

    The public register reads directly from the same system officers use to process cases. A controversial application can bring a surge of public visits during the consultation period, slowing or stopping the system officers rely on.

    Anti-pattern: consultation by email and PDF

    Consultation requests are sent to statutory consultees as PDF letters by email. Officers track response deadlines in spreadsheets, and copy consultee responses and suggested conditions back into the case by hand.

    Anti-pattern: assessing from documents, not data

    Officers re-read PDFs and check constraint maps by hand to find information the council already holds as data, such as the site boundary, constraints, and proposal details.

    Loading diagram…

    Best Practice: separate public access, and exchange data with consultees

    The public register should be served from a published copy of the case data, kept separate from the system officers use. However high public demand gets, it cannot affect officers' work.

    Where statutory consultees can accept it, consultation should be sent as structured data drawn from the application, through an integration or a shared case portal. Responses and suggested conditions should come back as data attached to the case, not as documents to re-key.

    Officers should assess from the same structured data, with the site boundary, constraints, and consultee responses already attached to the case.

    Real-world impact
    • Resilience

      Officers can keep working during high-profile consultations, even when public demand spikes.

    • Traceable consultation

      A consistent record of what was sent to each consultee, when, and what came back makes statutory deadlines easier to track and evidence.

    4 Determination and post-decision

    Phase 4 Architecture

    • Business Process
    • Anti-patterns
    • Target Architecture

    The process: issuing the final legal decision, recording conditions or reasons for refusal, notifying the applicant and interested parties, publishing the outcome as data to central government, and providing case information to the Planning Inspectorate if the decision is appealed.

    Relevant LGAM taxonomy nodes:

    DataDocument and records management IntegrationEvent-driven and streaming integration
    Anti-pattern: decision notices assembled by hand

    Officers copy conditions, including those suggested by consultees, from reports and emails into a document template to produce the decision notice. The decision then exists only as a PDF, and conditions have to be re-keyed later for monitoring and discharge.

    Anti-pattern: re-assembling the case for appeals

    When an appeal is lodged, officers download and re-bundle documents the council already holds, then send them to the Planning Inspectorate separately. This takes officer time and risks gaps or errors in what the Inspectorate receives.

    Loading diagram…

    Best practice: record the decision once, and reuse it

    The decision, conditions, and reasons should be recorded as structured data in case management. The decision notice is generated from that record, not typed separately.

    Recording the decision should trigger what follows: notifying the applicant and interested parties, updating the public register, and publishing decision data to the Planning Data Platform in line with data standards.

    If the decision is appealed, the case record and documents should be available to the Planning Inspectorate from the council's systems, without re-bundling.

    Real-world impact
    • Less administration

      Officers no longer re-key conditions or re-assemble documents the council already holds.

    • Consistent data

      Conditions recorded as data can be used for monitoring and discharge, and published decision data is consistent with the national standard.

    Target data models

    When procuring or configuring systems for Development Management, the architecture should align with the national data schemas maintained by MHCLG on planning.data.gov.uk. Treating these schemas as your target data model ensures automated interoperability with central government and neighbouring authorities.

    For policy context and current work on modernising planning services, see the MHCLG consultation on a data standard for planning applications and the Open Digital Planning programme.

    What makes planning technically different

    Every council domain has integration points with shared capabilities. Planning is architecturally distinctive in several ways that affect how you design those integrations:

    Volume and shape of unstructured data

    A single planning application can generate hundreds of documents — architectural drawings, structural calculations, environmental reports, public representations, consultee responses. These are overwhelmingly unstructured (PDFs, TIFFs, large-format plans). The ratio of unstructured to structured data is far higher than in most council domains. This means:

    • Document management integration cannot be purely metadata-and-reference — the volume and file sizes create real storage, retrieval and indexing challenges.
    • A corporate DMS built for committee papers may not handle the scale or the access patterns (frequent retrieval during assessment, public register obligations).
    • Search and classification of planning documents requires domain awareness that generic enterprise search may not provide.

    Spatial data is not a lookup — it is structurally integral

    In most council services, geospatial information is a reference — you look up an address, retrieve a UPRN, and move on. In planning, spatial data is structurally embedded in the case logic:

    • The application boundary (red-line) is a legal artefact that defines the scope of the permission.
    • Determination requires intersection of that boundary with constraint layers (conservation areas, flood zones, TPOs, Article 4 directions).
    • Changes to the boundary during assessment can change which policies apply and which consultees must be notified.
    • Integration with GIS is not read-only — the planning system may need to write or amend spatial objects.

    This makes the geospatial integration tighter and more bidirectional than most domains require. A simple "query the corporate GIS for constraints" pattern may not be sufficient if the planning system also needs to store, display and allow amendment of application-specific geometries.

    Statutory timescales create hard coupling to notifications

    Planning has legally defined determination periods (typically 8 or 13 weeks). Missing these deadlines has direct consequences — the applicant gains a right of appeal. This means:

    • Notifications (acknowledgements, consultation triggers, decision notices) are not fire-and-forget. The planning system needs to know they were sent and when.
    • If notification delivery fails silently, the statutory clock may expire without proper consultation having occurred — creating legal risk.
    • Integration with notification services needs confirmation of delivery and timestamps, not just acceptance of a message into a queue.

    Public register obligations

    Planning authorities must maintain a public register of applications and decisions. This is not the same as "publishing data" — it is a statutory requirement with specific content and accessibility expectations. This affects:

    • Document management — documents placed on the register must be redacted for personal information but remain publicly accessible.
    • Records management — the register is a permanent, legally authoritative record, not a retention-managed archive.
    • Data ownership — the planning system, the register, and the DMS may all hold overlapping copies of "the same" document, with different redaction states.
